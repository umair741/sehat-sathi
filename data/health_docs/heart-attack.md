# Heart Attack

Also called: Myocardial infarction, MI, Acute MI

Source: MedlinePlus (US National Library of Medicine) - https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/000195.htm

## What is a heart attack?

Most heart attacks are caused by a blood clot that blocks one of the coronary arteries. The coronary arteries bring blood and oxygen to the heart. If the blood flow is blocked, the heart is starved of oxygen and heart cells die. The medical term for this is myocardial infarction.

A heart attack is a MEDICAL EMERGENCY. If you have symptoms, call emergency services right away. DO NOT try to drive yourself to the hospital, and DO NOT WAIT - you are at greatest risk for sudden death in the early hours of a heart attack.

## Why does it happen? (Causes)

A substance called plaque can build up in the walls of your coronary arteries. This plaque is made up of cholesterol and other cells.

A heart attack most often occurs when a disruption in the plaque triggers blood platelets and other substances to form a blood clot at the site that blocks most or all of the oxygen-carrying blood from flowing to a part of the heart muscle.

A heart attack may occur:

- When you are resting or asleep
- After a sudden increase in physical activity
- When you are active outside in cold weather
- After sudden, severe emotional or physical stress, including an illness

Risk factors that lead to plaque buildup include smoking, high blood pressure, high cholesterol, diabetes, obesity, family history of heart disease, and age.

## What are the symptoms?

Chest pain is the most common symptom of a heart attack.

- You may feel the pain in only one part of your body, OR
- Pain may move from your chest to your arms, shoulder, neck, teeth, jaw, belly area, or back

The pain can be severe or mild. It can feel like:

- A tight band around the chest
- Bad indigestion
- Something heavy sitting on your chest
- Squeezing or heavy pressure

The pain most often lasts longer than 20 minutes. Rest and nitroglycerin may not completely relieve the pain. Symptoms may also go away and come back.

Other symptoms can include:

- Anxiety
- Cough
- Fainting
- Lightheadedness, dizziness
- Nausea and vomiting
- Palpitations (feeling like your heart is beating too fast or irregularly)
- Shortness of breath
- Sweating, which may be very heavy

Important: some people - including older adults, people with diabetes, and women - may have little or no chest pain, or atypical symptoms such as shortness of breath, fatigue, and weakness. A "silent heart attack" is a heart attack with no symptoms.

## How is it diagnosed?

Your provider will perform a physical exam and listen to your chest, and may find abnormal lung sounds (crackles), a heart murmur, a fast or uneven pulse, or abnormal blood pressure.

Tests include:

- Electrocardiogram (ECG) to look for heart damage - certain changes indicate a heart attack, although one can also occur without ECG changes
- Blood test to show heart tissue damage - this can confirm a heart attack and is often repeated over time
- Coronary angiography - uses special dye and x-rays to see how blood flows through your heart and helps decide which treatments you need

Other tests done while in the hospital:

- Echocardiography with or without stress testing
- Exercise stress test
- Nuclear stress test
- Heart CT scan or heart MRI

## What is the treatment? (Solution)

IMMEDIATE TREATMENT in the hospital:

- Heart monitor to watch how regularly your heart is beating
- Oxygen
- An intravenous line (IV) for medicines and fluids
- Nitroglycerin and morphine to help reduce chest pain
- Aspirin (or another blood-clot-preventing medicine if aspirin is not safe for you)
- Dangerous abnormal heartbeats (arrhythmias) may be treated with medicine or electric shocks

EMERGENCY PROCEDURES:

- Angioplasty - a procedure to open narrowed or blocked blood vessels. It should be done within 90 minutes of arriving at the hospital, and usually no later than 12 hours after a heart attack
- Stent - a small metal mesh tube placed during angioplasty that keeps the artery from closing again
- Thrombolytic therapy - medicines to break up the clot, best given soon after symptoms start, ideally within 30 minutes of arriving at the hospital
- Heart bypass surgery (coronary artery bypass grafting) - open heart surgery to reroute blood around blocked arteries

TREATMENT AFTER A HEART ATTACK:

You will likely need to take medicines, some for the rest of your life. NEVER stop or change medicines without talking to your provider - stopping certain medicines can be deadly.

Cardiac rehabilitation programs help most people recover and learn how to take heart medicines, eat a heart-healthy diet, exercise safely, and stop smoking.

## Outlook (Prognosis)

After a heart attack, you have a higher chance of having another one. How well you do depends on:

- The amount of damage to your heart muscle and heart valves
- Where the damage is located
- Your medical care after the heart attack

If your heart can no longer pump blood as well as it used to, you may develop heart failure. Abnormal heart rhythms can occur and can be life threatening.

Most people can slowly go back to normal activities, including sexual activity - talk to your provider about what is right for you.

## Can it be prevented?

- Do not smoke - and if you do, quit
- Control high blood pressure, high cholesterol, and diabetes
- Eat a heart-healthy diet
- Get regular exercise
- Stay at a healthy weight
- Manage stress
- Take heart medicines exactly as prescribed after a first heart attack to prevent another

## When to see a doctor

Call emergency services (in Pakistan: 1122) IMMEDIATELY if you have symptoms of a heart attack: chest pain or pressure lasting more than a few minutes, pain spreading to the arm, jaw, neck, or back, shortness of breath, sweating, nausea, or lightheadedness. Do not drive yourself to the hospital.
