# Food Poisoning

Also called: Foodborne illness

Source: MedlinePlus (US National Library of Medicine) - https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/001652.htm

## What is food poisoning?

Food poisoning occurs when you swallow food or water that contains bacteria, parasites, viruses, or the toxins made by these germs. Most cases are caused by common bacteria such as staphylococcus or E coli.

Food poisoning can affect one person or a group of people who all ate the same food. It is more common after eating at picnics, school cafeterias, large social functions, or restaurants.

## Why does it happen? (Causes)

When germs get into food, it is called contamination. This can happen in different ways:

- Meat or poultry comes into contact with bacteria from the intestines of an animal being processed
- Water used during growing or shipping contains animal or human waste
- Food is handled in an unsafe way during preparation in grocery stores, restaurants, or homes

Food poisoning can occur after eating or drinking:

- Any food prepared by someone who does not wash their hands properly
- Any food prepared using utensils, cutting boards, or other tools that are not fully cleaned
- Dairy products or food containing mayonnaise (such as coleslaw or potato salad) left out of the refrigerator too long
- Frozen or refrigerated foods not stored at the proper temperature, or not reheated to the right temperature
- Raw fish or oysters
- Raw fruits or vegetables that have not been washed well
- Unpasteurized juices and dairy products (look for the word "pasteurized")
- Undercooked meats or eggs
- Untreated water from a well or stream

Many types of germs and toxins may cause food poisoning, including:

- Campylobacter jejuni
- Cholera
- E coli
- Toxins in spoiled or tainted fish or shellfish
- Staphylococcus aureus
- Salmonella
- Shigella
- Yersinia

## Who is most at risk?

- Infants and older people are at the greatest risk
- People with serious medical conditions such as kidney disease, diabetes, cancer, or HIV/AIDS
- People with weakened immune systems
- People who travel to areas where they are exposed to germs that cause food poisoning
- Pregnant and breastfeeding women should use extra care to avoid food poisoning

## What are the symptoms?

Symptoms from the most common types of food poisoning often start within 2 to 6 hours of eating the food, though the timing may be shorter or longer depending on the cause.

Possible symptoms include:

- Abdominal cramps
- Diarrhea (may be bloody)
- Fever and chills
- Headache
- Nausea and vomiting
- Weakness (may be serious)

## How is it diagnosed?

Your provider will look for signs of food poisoning, such as stomach pain and signs of dehydration (too little fluid in the body).

Tests may be done on your stools or the food you ate to find out what type of germ is causing your symptoms - although tests may not always find the cause.

In more serious cases, a sigmoidoscopy may be done. This test uses a thin, hollow tube with a light to look inside the rectum and sigmoid colon for the source of bleeding or infection.

## What is the treatment? (Solution)

Most of the time, you will get better in a couple of days. The goal is to ease symptoms and make sure your body has the proper amount of fluids:

- Get plenty of rest
- Manage the diarrhea and control nausea and vomiting
- Drink oral rehydration mixtures to replace fluids and minerals lost through vomiting and diarrhea - oral rehydration powder can be purchased from a pharmacy; be sure to mix it in safe water
- You can make your own rehydration mixture: dissolve 1/2 teaspoon (3 g) salt, 1/2 teaspoon (2.3 g) baking soda, and 4 tablespoons (50 g) sugar in 4 1/4 cups (1 liter) of water
- If you have diarrhea and cannot drink or keep down fluids, you may need IV fluids - this is more common in young children
- If you take diuretics ("water pills") or ACE inhibitors for high blood pressure, contact your provider about whether to pause them while you have diarrhea - never stop or change medicines without asking
- For the most common causes of food poisoning, your provider will NOT prescribe antibiotics
- You can buy medicines that help slow diarrhea, but DO NOT use them without talking to your provider if you have bloody diarrhea, fever, or severe diarrhea, and DO NOT give them to children

## Outlook (Prognosis)

Most people fully recover from the most common types of food poisoning within 12 to 48 hours. Some types can cause serious complications. Death from food poisoning in people who are otherwise healthy is rare.

## Possible complications

Dehydration is the most common complication. Less common but much more serious complications depend on the bacteria causing the poisoning, and may include:

- Arthritis
- Bleeding problems
- Damage to the nervous system
- Kidney problems
- Swelling or irritation in the tissue around the heart

## Can it be prevented?

- Wash hands thoroughly with soap before preparing or eating food
- Keep utensils, cutting boards, and kitchen surfaces clean
- Cook meats and eggs thoroughly; avoid undercooked foods
- Store food at proper temperatures and refrigerate leftovers promptly
- Wash raw fruits and vegetables well
- Drink only treated, boiled, or bottled water when the water supply is not safe
- Avoid raw dairy and unpasteurized products
- When traveling, eat well-cooked food and drink bottled or boiled water

## When to see a doctor

Contact your provider if you have:

- Blood or pus in your stools
- Diarrhea and inability to drink fluids due to nausea and vomiting
- A fever above 101°F (38.3°C), or your child has a fever above 100.4°F (38°C) along with diarrhea
- Signs of dehydration (thirst, dizziness, lightheadedness)
- Recently traveled to a foreign country and developed diarrhea
- Diarrhea that has not improved in 5 days (2 days for an infant or child), or has gotten worse
- A child who has been vomiting for more than 12 hours (for a newborn under 3 months, call as soon as vomiting or diarrhea begins)
- Suspected food poisoning from mushrooms (potentially fatal), fish or other seafood, or botulism (also potentially fatal)
