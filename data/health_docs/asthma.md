# Asthma

Also called: Bronchial asthma

Source: MedlinePlus (US National Library of Medicine) - https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/000141.htm

## What is asthma?

Asthma is a chronic disease that causes the airways of the lungs to swell and become narrow. It leads to breathing difficulty such as wheezing, shortness of breath, chest tightness, and coughing.

Asthma symptoms vary from person to person - you may have symptoms all the time or mostly during physical activity. Most people with asthma have attacks separated by symptom-free periods. Asthma attacks can last for minutes to days and may start suddenly or develop slowly. An attack may become dangerous if airflow is severely blocked.

## Why does it happen? (Causes)

Asthma is caused by swelling (inflammation) in the airways. An asthma attack occurs when the lining of the air passages becomes swollen and the muscles surrounding the airways become tight. This narrowing reduces the amount of air that can pass through the airway.

Asthma symptoms can be triggered by breathing in allergens or other triggers. Common asthma triggers include:

- Animals (pet hair or dander)
- Dust mites
- Certain medicines (such as aspirin and other NSAIDs)
- Changes in weather (most often cold weather)
- Chemicals in the air (pollution) or in food
- Physical activity
- Mold
- Pollen
- Respiratory infections, such as the common cold and many other viruses
- Strong emotions (stress)
- Tobacco or other inhalant that is smoked

Substances in some workplaces can also trigger asthma symptoms (occupational asthma) - the most common are wood dust, grain dust, animal dander, fungi, or chemicals.

Many people with asthma have a personal or family history of allergies, such as hay fever (allergic rhinitis) or eczema.

## What are the symptoms?

Symptoms of asthma include:

- Cough with or without phlegm (sputum) production
- Pulling in of the skin between the ribs when breathing (intercostal retractions)
- Shortness of breath that gets worse with exercise or activity
- Whistling sound or wheezing as you breathe
- Pain or tightness in the chest
- Difficulty sleeping
- Abnormal breathing pattern (breathing out takes more than twice as long as breathing in)

EMERGENCY symptoms that need prompt medical help:

- Bluish color to the lips and face (cyanosis)
- Decreased level of alertness, such as severe drowsiness or confusion, during an attack
- Extreme difficulty breathing
- Rapid pulse
- Severe anxiety due to shortness of breath
- Sweating
- Difficulty speaking
- Breathing temporarily stops

## How is it diagnosed?

Your provider will listen to your lungs with a stethoscope (wheezing or other asthma-related sounds may be heard) and take your medical history.

Tests that may be ordered include:

- Lung function tests, including peak flow measurements
- Allergy testing - a skin test or blood test to see if you are allergic to certain substances
- Arterial blood gas - often done during a severe asthma attack
- Chest x-ray or CT scan - mainly to check for other conditions, as asthma attacks often occur with a normal chest x-ray

## What is the treatment? (Solution)

The goals of treatment are to control airway swelling, limit exposure to triggers, and help you do normal activities without symptoms.

MEDICINES FOR ASTHMA - two kinds:

1. Long-term (control) medicines - taken every day to PREVENT attacks, even when you feel OK. Some are inhaled (such as steroids and long-acting beta-agonists); others are taken by mouth.

2. Quick-relief (rescue) medicines - used DURING attacks for coughing, wheezing, or trouble breathing, and just before physical activity to prevent symptoms. These include short-acting inhaled bronchodilators and oral corticosteroids for severe attacks.

Tell your provider if you use quick-relief medicines twice a week or more - your asthma may not be under control, and your treatment may need to change.

A severe asthma attack requires a visit to your provider and possibly a hospital stay, with oxygen, breathing assistance, and medicines given through a vein (IV) or by a nebulizer.

ASTHMA CARE AT HOME:

- Know the asthma symptoms to watch for
- Know how to take your peak flow reading and what it means - a peak flow meter measures how quickly you can move air out of your lungs; values of 50% to 80% of your best result signal a moderate attack, below 50% a severe attack
- Know which triggers make your asthma worse and what to do when exposed
- Follow a written asthma action plan: medicines to take when stable, your trigger list, how to recognize worsening asthma, and when to contact your provider

## Outlook (Prognosis)

There is no cure for asthma, although symptoms sometimes improve over time, particularly in children. With proper self-care and medical treatment, most people with asthma can lead a normal life.

## Possible complications

The complications of asthma can be severe, and may include:

- Death
- Decreased ability to exercise and take part in other activities
- Lack of sleep due to nighttime symptoms
- Permanent changes in the function of the lungs
- Persistent cough
- Trouble breathing that requires a ventilator

## Can it be prevented?

You can reduce asthma symptoms by avoiding triggers:

- Cover bedding with allergy-proof casings to reduce dust mites
- Remove carpets from bedrooms and vacuum regularly
- Use only unscented detergents and cleaning materials
- Keep humidity levels low and fix leaks to reduce mold growth
- Keep the house clean and food in containers and out of bedrooms - cockroach body parts and droppings can trigger attacks
- Keep pets out of the bedroom if someone is allergic
- Eliminate tobacco smoke from the home - this is the single most important thing a family can do; smoking outside is not enough because smoke residue travels inside on clothes and hair
- Avoid air pollution, industrial dust, and irritating fumes as much as possible

## When to see a doctor

Contact your provider if asthma symptoms develop, if an attack requires more medicine than recommended, if symptoms get worse or do not improve with treatment, or if you have shortness of breath while talking.

Go to the emergency room or call emergency services (in Pakistan: 1122) right away for: drowsiness or confusion, severe shortness of breath at rest, peak flow below 50% of personal best, severe chest pain, bluish lips and face, extreme difficulty breathing, rapid pulse, or severe anxiety due to shortness of breath.
