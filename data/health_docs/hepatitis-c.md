# Hepatitis C

Also called: HCV infection, Hep C

Source: MedlinePlus (US National Library of Medicine) - https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/000284.htm

## What is hepatitis C?

Hepatitis C is a viral disease that leads to swelling (inflammation) of the liver. It is caused by the hepatitis C virus (HCV). Other types of viral hepatitis include hepatitis A, hepatitis B, hepatitis D, and hepatitis E.

Most people who are recently infected with HCV do not have symptoms, and chronic infection often causes no symptoms until the liver becomes scarred (cirrhosis). Left untreated, chronic hepatitis C can lead to liver failure or liver cancer. With modern treatment, most people can be cured.

## Why does it happen? (Causes)

You can catch hepatitis C if the blood of a person who has HCV enters your body. Exposure may occur:

- After a needle stick or sharps injury
- If blood from someone who has HCV contacts a cut on your skin, or contacts your eyes or mouth
- While injecting street drugs
- After a blood transfusion (rare in countries that screen donated blood)

Hepatitis C is NOT spread by casual contact such as holding hands, kissing, coughing or sneezing, breastfeeding, or sharing eating utensils or drinking glasses.

## Who is more likely to get it?

People at risk for HCV include those who:

- Inject street drugs or share a needle with someone who has HCV
- Have been on long-term kidney dialysis
- Have regular contact with blood at work (such as health care workers)
- Have unprotected sexual contact with a person who has HCV
- Were born to a mother who had HCV
- Received a tattoo or acupuncture with needles that were not disinfected properly after being used on another person
- Received an organ transplant from a donor who has HCV
- Share personal items, such as toothbrushes and razors, with someone who has HCV (less common)

## What are the symptoms?

Most people who are recently infected have no symptoms. Some people have yellowing of the skin (jaundice). Chronic infection often causes fatigue, abdominal pain, depression, and other problems.

The following symptoms may occur with HCV infection:

- Pain in the right upper abdomen
- Abdominal swelling due to fluid (ascites)
- Clay-colored or pale stools
- Dark urine
- Fatigue
- Fever
- Itching
- Jaundice (yellowing of the skin and eyes)
- Loss of appetite
- Nausea and vomiting

Many people have no symptoms at all until their liver becomes scarred (cirrhosis).

## How is it diagnosed?

Blood tests are done to check for HCV:

- Enzyme immunoassay (EIA) to detect HCV antibody (anti-HCV)
- Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) to detect the virus itself, to measure virus levels (viral load), and to identify the type of virus

All adults ages 18 to 79 should get a one-time test for HCV. If the antibody test is positive, a PCR test is used to confirm the infection.

Further genetic testing is done to check the type of HCV (genotype). There are six types of the virus (genotypes 1 through 6), and the results help your provider choose the best treatment for you.

Tests done to identify and monitor liver damage from HCV include:

- Albumin level
- Liver function tests
- Prothrombin time
- Liver biopsy
- Transient elastography using ultrasound
- Magnetic resonance elastography (MRE)

## What is the treatment? (Solution)

Antiviral medicines are used to treat HCV. The goal of treatment is to rid the body of the virus, which prevents the liver damage that may lead to liver failure or liver cancer. Treatment is especially important for people who are showing signs of liver fibrosis or scarring.

Newer antiviral medicines:

- Provide an excellent cure rate
- Have fewer side effects and are easier to take than older medicines
- Are taken by mouth for 8 to 24 weeks

The choice of medicine depends on the genotype of HCV you have.

A liver transplant may be recommended for people who develop cirrhosis or liver cancer.

If you have HCV:

- Do not take over-the-counter medicines, vitamins, or supplements without asking your provider first
- Do not use alcohol or street drugs - alcohol speeds up liver damage and reduces how well medicines work
- If blood tests show you do not have antibodies to hepatitis A and B, get vaccinated against hepatitis A and hepatitis B

## Outlook (Prognosis)

Most people (75% to 85%) who are infected with the virus develop chronic HCV unless they get treatment. Chronic HCV poses a risk for cirrhosis and liver cancer.

A good response to treatment occurs when the virus can no longer be detected in the blood 12 weeks or more after treatment. This is called a "sustained virologic response" (SVR). Up to 90% of those treated have this response.

Some people do not respond to the initial treatment and may need to be re-treated with a different class of medicines. It is also possible to become re-infected with a different genotype of HCV.

## Can it be prevented?

Currently there is no vaccine for HCV. To help prevent the spread from person to person:

- Health care workers should follow precautions when handling blood
- Do not share needles with anyone
- Do not get tattoos or body piercings or receive acupuncture from someone who does not have a permit or license
- Do not share personal items, such as razors and toothbrushes
- Practice safer sex

If you or your sexual partner has HCV and you have been in a stable, monogamous relationship, the risk of passing the virus between you is low.

## When to see a doctor

Contact your provider if:

- You develop symptoms of hepatitis
- You believe you have been exposed to HCV (for example, a needle stick injury or shared needle use)
